ReputableChat rules, version 0.001

These rules define each record, its fields, and the rules for validating it. This document is the rules field of the genesis record, which is the developer's identity declaration. Whenever the rules change, a release record carrying the new rules is added to the chain, and every later record must conform to it. These rules distinguish between rules that are enforced and guidelines which are not. A guideline is a suggestion: a record that ignores one is still accepted, but clients will likely ignore the nonconforming values.

1. RECORDS

A record is a payload and a signature over it.

Payload
  A JSON object in canonical form: keys sorted, no whitespace, UTF-8, and floating-point numbers recorded as a string surrounded by double quotes.

Signature
  Ed25519 over the canonical bytes of the payload, made by the key in the payload's pubkey or mpubkey field, written as base64url (64 bytes). A record carrying both is valid if the signature verifies against either.

Record hash
  SHA-256 over the canonical payload, a newline, and the signature, written as 64 lowercase hex characters. It is the record's identity. Canonical JSON and base64url never contain a raw newline, and base64url is written without padding.

Text
  UTF-8 with surrounding whitespace removed. It contains no control characters other than tab, newline and carriage return. Each field gives its own length limit, counted in bytes.

Decimal
  A number written as a string: an optional minus sign, a whole part with no leading zeros, and optionally a point followed by one to eighteen digits, the last of which is not zero, such as "0.5" or "-1". Zero is written "0", never "-0". Each number has exactly one spelling.

File
  Files are too big to include on the chain, so a record refers to one by the SHA-256 of its bytes in hex plus a file extension. The chain does not verify whether the file can be found, whether it matches its extension, or whether the extension is a known one; it only verifies that the extension is 1 to 5 lowercase letters or digits.

Key
  A public key written as base64url (32 bytes): Ed25519 for a key that signs, X25519 for a key that receives encrypted messages.

Timestamp
  A integer value representing whole seconds since 1970-01-01 00:00 UTC.

Validity
  Whether a record is valid is decided by the record and the history it acknowledges, and by nothing else. So its author can calculate it before signing, every reader reaches the same answer, and no record that arrives later can make an earlier one invalid.

2. COMMON FIELDS

Unless otherwise stated each field is required. Some optional fields may be required by certain record types. Each field names in brackets the kind of value it holds, as defined in section 1; [Record hashes] is a list of them.

Record type (type) [Text]
  Three or more parts separated by colons. The first is always "reputablechat". The second is one of: attestation, heartbeat, identity, message, notice, reaction or release. The third is the rules version the record conforms to. Any further parts are ignored by the chain. The whole type is at most 128 bytes. Records must conform to the newest rules they acknowledge; a record whose version does not match those rules is invalid.

  Attestation: A public statement of how reliable other users are. Example: reputablechat:attestation:v0.001
  Heartbeat: Nothing but acks, joining many records at once. Example: reputablechat:heartbeat:v0.001
  Identity: A public declaration of identity, including a preferred handle and the account's keys. Example: reputablechat:identity:v0.001
  Message: A message to be displayed on the network. Example: reputablechat:message:v0.001
  Notice: An alert posted to the network, which may or may not be displayed to users. Example: reputablechat:notice:v0.001
  Reaction: A reaction to another record on the network, such as a like. Example: reputablechat:reaction:v0.001
  Release: A published version of the client. Example: reputablechat:release:v0.001

Account ID (id) [Record hash]
  The record hash of an account's first identity declaration. It is used to identify the account. This field is omitted only in the first identity declaration.

Working key (pubkey) [Key]
  Optional, but required when the record is signed with the account's working key, and on every first identity declaration, which declares it. A record carries pubkey, mpubkey, or both, and is signed with one of the keys it carries. A record carrying neither is invalid. The working key is the key an account uses day to day, as opposed to its master key.

  An account's keys, as seen by a record, are the working key and master public key set by its first identity declaration, as replaced by the key-change and master-key-change notices that the record acknowledges, directly or through the records it acknowledges. A record that was valid stays valid after the account changes keys.

  A key change is endorsed once a quorum record names it, and tentative until then. An account's current keys, as seen by a record, are therefore its last endorsed keys and any tentative set on top of them. A record must be signed with one of its account's current keys: the tentative keys, so that a changed key works at once rather than waiting on anybody, or the last endorsed keys, so that a key change cannot lock out the person a stolen key was taken from. A record signed with any other key is invalid and rejected.

  A record signed with the last endorsed key that acknowledges a tentative change replacing that key contests the change, and makes the account disputed (see Disputed in section 7). A record that does not acknowledge the change makes no such claim: a second client that has not yet caught up is signing with a key it has every reason to think current, not contesting anything.

  Guideline: an adjudicator asked to settle a contested key change is expected to wait to endorse it until it can verify that the account's owner made it.

Ack (ack) [Record hashes]
  The record hashes of the most recent records the author had seen and chose to acknowledge: sorted, with no duplicates, at most 16 -- or, in a heartbeat, as many as fit in 1,048,576 bytes. Empty in the genesis record and nowhere else. Every record it names must share this record's version, unless this is a release record. Following acks back from any record always leads to the genesis record.

Body (body) [Text]
  Text, at most 16,000 bytes, and may be empty. It can carry a map, or plain text. The chain checks only that it is Text, so it can equally carry structured data, such as a JSON object.

Rules (rules) [Text]
  Text, 1 to 64,000 bytes: a complete rules document, such as this one. Required on the genesis record and on every release record, and not allowed on any other record.

Record timestamp (ts) [Timestamp]
  The time the author says they signed, in whole seconds since 1970-01-01 00:00 UTC. It is not verified, but it must conform to certain criteria when it is in a heartbeat record.

Target (target) [Record hashes]
  Optional. A list of record hashes of existing records on the chain that this record replies to, comments on or reacts to: sorted, with no duplicates, at most 256.

Endorse (endorse) [Record hashes]
  Optional. A list of record hashes the author votes for: sorted, with no duplicates, at most 256. Endorsing is a vote, not a review: most endorsements are cast automatically, by software following rules its owner set, rather than by a person who has read the record. Every record it names must already be in this record's history, through its ack, so nobody can vote for a record that is not in their history. It is not an ack: an ack says what the author had seen, and endorse says which of those records the author votes for. The chain acts on it only where these rules say so, and ignores it everywhere else.

Transfer (transfer) [Object]
  Optional. Moves currency, as set out in section 10. An object holding out, and optionally currency and in:

  currency: the account ID of the currency being moved. Omitted when it is the author's own, which is also how a first identity declaration, having no account ID yet, names its own.
  in: the outputs this record spends, each written as the record hash of the record that made it: sorted, with no duplicates, at most 256. A record makes at most one output for each account, so the record hash and this record's author together name the output being spent, and it is spent whole. Omitted when the author is issuing new currency, which only a transfer of the author's own currency may do.
  out: the outputs this record makes, 1 to 256 of them, sorted by to, an output without to coming first, with no two going to the same account and at most one without to. Each holds value, a decimal greater than zero, and to, the account ID it goes to. An output without to destroys its value.

  The values in out add up to exactly the values of the outputs named in in, unless in is omitted. A record whose transfer does not add up is invalid.

Title (title) [Text]
  Optional. Text, 1 to 280 bytes.

File (file) [File]
  Optional. A list of files, as defined above, that the record refers to: in the author's order, with no duplicates, at most 16.

Url (url) [Text]
  Optional. Text, 1 to 2,048 bytes, containing no whitespace. The network does not check that it is a URL or restrict its scheme.

Language (lang) [Text]
  Optional. Text, 1 to 35 bytes of letters, digits and hyphens: the language the record is written in.

  Guideline: a BCP 47 language tag, such as "en" or "pt-BR".

Master public key (mpubkey) [Key]
  Optional, but required when the record is signed with the master key. Designed to provide an emergency method of recovering the account in the event the working keys become compromised. A first identity declaration that carries it declares the account's master key, and may be signed with either key. The master public key can only be changed by a master-key-change notice signed with the current master key.

3. IDENTITY DECLARATION RECORDS

An identity declaration requires title, which holds the handle, and may also carry an avatar, a bio, an encryption key and a list of adjudicators.

Handle (title)
  The network identifies each user by their account ID, which is neither pleasant to look at nor easy to read. So each user chooses a handle to be known by, 1 to 64 bytes. Handles are not unique: the first few characters of the account ID are shown beside a handle, as a kind of last name.

Avatar (file)
  The first file in the list: an image, named by the SHA-256 of its bytes in hex plus a file extension. Images are expected to be 128 x 128 with a common image extension. The network does not enforce either, but most servers will likely ignore images that do not conform.

Bio (body)
  Text about the user. The network limits it only as it limits any body; each app decides how much of it to show.

Encryption key (epubkey) [Key]
  Optional. An X25519 public key, for anyone who wants to send this account a message only it can read. The messages themselves are not records; the chain only says which key is current, which, as for every field of an identity declaration, is the one on the latest declaration the reader's record acknowledges. A declaration without one says the account has no encryption key. A key change does not change it: an account that changes its working key, even one that was stolen, publishes a new encryption key when it chooses to.

  Guideline: derived from the same seed as the working key, so that it can be recovered with the account.

Adjudicators (adjudicators)
  Optional. A list of account IDs, in the author's order, with no duplicates, at most 16. They are the accounts this account appoints to settle which key change it accepts, should it ever be disputed. An absent or empty list names the developer's account alone.

  This is the one field a new identity declaration does not change by itself. A declaration carrying a different list takes effect in every other field at once, while the list last endorsed stays in force until a quorum record names the new one. Any key that can sign for the account could otherwise appoint the accounts that would later decide who owns it.

  Guideline: an adjudicator is expected to endorse regularly, so that an account is never left disputed for want of an answer, and to wait long enough before endorsing a key change that the account's owner has a fair chance to notice and dispute it. How long that is, and whether to endorse by hand or after a quiet interval, is each adjudicator's own business; an account may name several, and drop one that answers too slowly or too fast.

The genesis record is the one identity declaration that carries rules. Its body is the genesis account's bio, like anyone else's.

4. ATTESTATION RECORDS

An attestation requires one additional field, scores, and may also carry derived.

Scores (scores)
  A map from each rated user's account ID to the author's opinion of them. Each value holds reputation and trust, both decimals from -1 to +1. Reputation is the author's opinion of the user's messages; trust is the author's opinion of the user's attestations. Trust is expected to be 0 or 1 in most cases. A 0 marks a user who is honest but too trusting of bad actors: their own messages are still seen, but their attestations count for nothing, so nobody becomes visible on their recommendation alone.

Derived (derived)
  The same format as scores, holding the reputation and trust the author has calculated for every user within N hops. The author calculates them with their own formulas, standard or custom. Any value is accepted, and has no effect on the record's validity, as long as it lies between -1 and +1 and each key is an account ID.

5. MESSAGE RECORDS

The network gives a message's content no meaning. A message follows the common rules and the rules of each field it carries, and nothing more; what it means is for whoever reads it.

6. REACTION RECORDS

A reaction requires target: the record or records reacted to. Any record type may be the target, and multiple record types can be targeted by a single reaction record.

Guideline: a body of the form :<image name>: is a custom emoji, where the image name is the SHA-256 of the image's bytes in hex plus a file extension, as for an avatar. Clients show the image if their server has it.

7. NOTICE RECORDS

A notice requires one additional field, kind: text, 1 to 64 bytes. The kinds below have a meaning to the network. A notice of any other kind is accepted, but the network itself ignores it. It is expected servers will use their own kind definitions to indicate outages, or other information relevant to the server.

Compromised (kind: compromised)
  Says that a key of the account named by id has been compromised. Signed with one of that account's current keys, or posted by one of its adjudicators. Its target names the records its author holds to be fraudulent. The chain does not act on that list: it is evidence for the adjudicators and for readers, so that naming a record cannot be used to take it off the chain. It makes the account disputed.

Disputed
  Two records are concurrent when neither acknowledges the other, directly or through the records it acknowledges. Two concurrent records from one account conflict when both are key changes or master key changes, whatever either of them says, or when both spend the same output (section 10).

  An account is disputed, as seen by a record, when that record acknowledges, and no later quorum record for the account, any of: a compromised notice for it; two of its own records that conflict; five of its own records none of which acknowledges any of the others; or one of its own records signed with its last endorsed key that acknowledges a tentative change replacing that key.

  That last one is the same event seen from the other side: a key still signing after it was replaced is the previous holder saying the replacement was not theirs. None of the four needs anybody to notice and report it, because the records say it themselves. A stranger holding a stolen key and an owner forking their own history over a key or an output cannot be told apart from the chain, so both are treated as one. Disputed says only that the account's records wait on a decision, not whose fault it is. Whichever record makes an account disputed is its disputing record: the notice, the later of two conflicting records to reach a reader, the fifth concurrent record to reach a reader, or the record signed with the replaced key.

  A disputing record may acknowledge the records it contests, including a record from the account it is about. Nothing it acknowledges was published after it, so the rule below does not reach them.

  A record from a disputed account takes no effect while the account stays disputed. The conflicting records are the exception: they are what is being decided between. The key change a quorum record names takes effect when it does, and a conflicting spend takes effect as section 10 sets out.

  A record is invalid if it directly acks a record from a disputed account that the account published after, or concurrently with, the disputing record, where that disputing record is in the acking record's own history. Acking such a record indirectly is not affected, or the invalidity would spread through the chain until nothing was left valid. A quorum record may ack the record it names, and is the only record that may: that is how an accepted key change enters the history, so that records acknowledging the quorum see the account's new key.

  Up to four records from one account that do not conflict, none of which acknowledges any of the others, are ordinary. Two clients belonging to one person will sometimes publish at the same moment without either seeing the other, and some records are published on a schedule rather than by a person. Each account is expected to be run by one person or one program at a time, so four is a buffer rather than an allowance: five records none of which saw the others is not somebody on two devices.

Key change (kind: key-change)
  Moves an account to a new working key. Signed with the account's current master key or current working key. The body is the new working key in base64url without padding, and nothing else.

  Where a key change signed with the master key and one signed with the working key disagree, the master key's wins, whichever was published first. The working key is the master key's delegate and cannot overrule it.

Master key change (kind: master-key-change)
  Moves an account to a new master key. Signed with the account's current master key. The body is the new master public key in base64url without padding, and nothing else.

Quorum (kind: quorum)
  Ends an account's disputed state by settling which of its key changes the account's adjudicators accept. Its target names that key change, and its endorse field names the records in which the adjudicators endorsed it. Where the account was disputed without competing key changes, its target instead names the compromised notice, or one of the records that made the account disputed, and the account carries on with the keys it had. If those keys really were stolen, the owner changes them afterwards, and a thief who contests that change starts a new dispute for a new quorum. Anyone may post one: it decides nothing itself, it only states a count anybody can check.

  It is valid when more than half of the account's adjudicators have endorsed the record it names, counting each adjudicator once. An adjudicator that endorsed more than one of the competing key changes is counted in neither total and not counted in the half. Where no record ever reaches that number the account stays disputed.

  The record it names becomes endorsed, and where it is a key change every other change of the same kind that was tentative when the quorum was signed becomes void: neither endorsed nor tentative, and no longer a key the account can sign with. Endorsing one of two competing changes without voiding the other would leave the account forked, and so disputed, by the very record meant to settle it. A change published after the quorum is a fresh one and starts tentative like any other.

  An adjudicator endorses by naming the record in the endorse field of any record it signs. The record must be in the adjudicator's history, as for any endorsement (section 2), so the adjudicator acks it, and leaves the competing record out of its history: the rule above forbids acking a record from a disputed account only to a record whose own history holds the dispute. Endorse is still a field of its own, because an ack says only what the author has seen, and the adjudicator is voting for one of the records it has seen.

8. RELEASE RECORDS

A release record marks the start of a change to the rules, and carries the new rules in its rules field; its body may say what changed. It is the only record whose ack may name records of an older version. Every record in its ack must be of the same previous version, and every record that names the release in its ack must be of the release's version.

9. HEARTBEAT RECORDS

A heartbeat is nothing but acks and the pace its publisher promises to keep. It carries no title, file, url, transfer or target, and its body is empty. Anyone may publish one; in practice servers will, because a server sees the whole frontier and an ordinary account sees what it is shown.

What it is for is joining. An ordinary record acknowledges at most 16 others, so branches of the history rejoin only as fast as ordinary traffic happens to weave them together. A heartbeat acknowledges as much of the frontier as it can reach, which does three things at once: it collapses concurrency, so two records published moments apart are ordered rather than concurrent soon after; it shortens every walk, since one hop through a heartbeat reaches everything that heartbeat reached; and it does both without asking every message to carry a megabyte of hashes.

  It must directly ack the author's previous heartbeat, where it has published one, so that each publisher's heartbeats form a chain and there is no question which came before which. A publisher's first heartbeat has none to name.

  Its ts must be greater than that of the heartbeat it acks, and at least 480 seconds later. A minimum only: a publisher that goes down and comes back an hour later has not broken a rule, and one that is pushed to post on a fixed beat would be pushed to invent timestamps to meet it.

  Guideline: the chain cannot check a clock, but a server can. It is expected to refuse records whose ts is far from its own time, and a publisher whose heartbeats claim a pace no server believes will simply stop being relayed.

Within (within)
  Required in a heartbeat and allowed in no other record. The greatest number of seconds the publisher intends to leave before its next heartbeat: a whole number, at least 480, which is the least the rules allow between one publisher's heartbeats. There is no upper bound. A publisher that promises little is followed by nobody, and that is the network's business rather than the chain's.

Waiting (waiting)
  Required in a heartbeat and allowed in no other record. The greatest number of records the publisher intends to leave waiting, unjoined by any heartbeat of its own, before publishing the next one without waiting out its within: a whole number, at least 1. There is no upper bound here either; a number larger than one heartbeat's ack can hold is a promise its publisher cannot keep, which readers can see for themselves.

  Both are ceilings, and either brings the next heartbeat. Seconds alone would leave a burst of traffic concurrent for as long as the publisher waited, whatever arrived in it; records alone would leave a publisher that has stopped looking no different from a network that has gone quiet.

  The chain checks the fields and not the promise, which a reader can check for itself: each heartbeat states the terms its successor is expected to meet, and a publisher's heartbeats are chained, so the promise and the record of keeping it sit next to each other.

  Guideline: within 600 seconds, with waiting set high enough that the seconds are the usual trigger and the records the exception. A publisher on a quiet server may promise a longer within instead and let waiting bring its heartbeats forward when the traffic warrants.

Note what a heartbeat does not need to be. It is a shortcut rather than a duty, so a frontier wider than one heartbeat can hold is no failure: what it leaves out is still reachable through ordinary records, and several publishers cover overlapping parts of it. A record joined to the live history by a heartbeat stops being concurrent with anything after it.

Stale

Publishing is joining. An account that signs records while nothing reaches it, and a publisher that stops naming the backbone it had been naming, are both leaving the one history, and both say so in their own records. The rules below are the whole of it: they are decided by a record and the history it acknowledges, like every other rule here, so an author can see the answer before signing and every reader reaches the same one. Nobody has to notice anything.

  A record is invalid if its history holds 256 or more records by its author that no heartbeat by another account in that history acknowledges. Any heartbeat by anybody else clears the count, which is a low bar on a network of any size and deliberately so: it is the difference between taking part and talking to oneself.

  The publishers a heartbeat is following are the accounts whose heartbeats its author's last four heartbeats name. A heartbeat is invalid if, for half of them or more, it does not name a heartbeat later than the latest of that publisher's that those four named. A publisher that stops, or is cut off, is a minority its followers can carry; a group of publishers that abandons the rest of the backbone while carrying on among itself says exactly that in its own heartbeats, where the heartbeats it names stand still while its own march on.

  A record is invalid if it directly acks a record that is invalid under either rule above. Directly only: a record that acks a record that acked a stale one is unaffected, or one account leaving would take with it everything that had ever acknowledged it. Acking a record that left is choosing the same branch one step later, and nothing forces the choice -- the records are there to be read before a reader acks them.

  Note what this does not do. It does not decide which branch is the real one, because nothing here can: it says only that a branch which stops joining the other is invalid to both sides at once, by arithmetic either side can do. A group that leaves has removed itself, and the accounts that followed it can carry their identities to a server that stayed, because an account is its keys rather than its host.

10. CURRENCY

Any account may also be a currency, named by its account ID and issued by that account, its issuer. Most accounts never are. One that is might be a server's, selling its currency to pay for what the server provides, or a person keeping track of what they are owed. What a currency is worth and what it buys are its issuer's business; the chain records who holds it and nothing else.

Becoming a currency
  An account becomes a currency with an identity declaration, its first or a later one, carrying a transfer of its own currency. As seen by a record, an account is a currency when such a declaration is in that record's history, or is that record.

Outputs
  Currency is held in outputs. Each entry in a transfer's out list is one output, of that transfer's currency, belonging to the account in its to. An output is named by the record hash of the record that made it together with the account it belongs to, since a record makes at most one output for each account. It is spent whole, by a record from the account it belongs to naming it in in; what the spender means to keep goes back to the spender as a new output, as change.

Issuing
  An issuer holds an unlimited amount of its own currency. A transfer of its own currency with in omitted issues new currency: its outputs come from nothing. A currency may issue in any record it signs. Every transfer that spends anything, the issuer's own included, makes exactly what it spends; what an issuer means to destroy goes in an output without to.

Validity
  A record carrying a transfer is invalid if the account its currency names is not a currency as seen by that record; if a to names an account, other than the author's own, whose first identity declaration is not in the record's history; if the values do not add up as above; or if any output in its in list is not in the record's history, belongs to another account, is of another currency, or is already named in the in list of a record in the record's history. So an output can be spent once in any one history, and the only way to spend it twice is in two records neither of which acknowledges the other.

Double spends
  Two such records conflict, and make the account that signed them disputed (section 7). Each may be valid, since neither's history contains the other, and neither takes effect until one is chosen. The currency's issuer chooses, whoever the spender's adjudicators are: it endorses the one it accepts, in the endorse field of any record it signs, which must be in its history, and that one takes effect; the other never does. An issuer that endorses more than one of them has chosen none. Where the spender is the issuer itself, its own adjudicators choose instead, by a quorum record naming the spend that takes effect.

  An output made by a record that takes no effect does not exist, and a spend of it takes no effect either.
